# Ladbad cards: rules, Love it list, Whack list

This file is the brief for writing new cards. The rules come from Yashant's practice-round votes on 12 Nov 2026: 160 cards voted, 111 Love it and 49 Whack. All 49 Whack cards were removed from the game. Both lists are kept below as examples.

## What the votes showed

**Short cards lost and long cards won.** This was the clearest signal:

| Tier | Love it | Whack | Whacked |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | 11 | 16 | 59% |
| Slippery | 21 | 17 | 45% |
| Brain melt | 32 | 10 | 24% |
| Boss | 27 | 1 | 4% |
| Impossible | 20 | 5 | 20% |

**Sentences people say at home won. One-word names lost.** In the "Heard at home" category 16 cards were loved and 2 whacked. In "Everyday talk" 15 were loved and 5 whacked. Single-word foods (12 loved, 14 whacked) and single-word places (21 loved, 15 whacked) were whacked the most. A famous name like Pokhara or Chitwan, once shown in a category, is guessed before the joke can land.

**Why the 49 lost.** Each one fits one of four patterns. The tag next to each card in the Whack list says which.

1. **Chopped spelling (23 cards).** The romanised word is cut into pieces and given punctuation: *Tea? Harr!*, *Choy? Lah!*, *Pud, Ha!*, *Boot? Wall!*. The pieces barely count as English and build no picture, so the reader is really reading the answer aloud in a funny voice. There is nothing to crack.
2. **One-word sound-alike (5 cards).** *Loafing*, *Tamale*, *But Moss*, *Gee Ow*, *Cheetah Won*. Either the room gets it at once, or it is just a word with nowhere to go.
3. **Gives the answer away (10 cards).** A piece of the answer, or another Nepali word, sits in plain sight: *Phew* (Phewa), *Saga* (Sagar), *Penny* (pani), *cup* and *Cheer* (ek cup chiya), *Ghee*, *Amma*, *Dee Dee* (didi). *Race Ham Ferrari* falls here too: the song is so famous that the first two words give it away.
4. **Word salad or loose sound (11 cards).** These are long cards built from fragments that make no sense together (*Up, who? Bah! Lotta jug?*) or that drift off the real sounds (*A cup on Thaddeus' car, Jay!* does not say "ek pantha dui kaaj"). Players hear noise, not a phrase.

## Rules for new cards

Check every new card against all of these.

1. **Start on the answer's first sound.** Never add a syllable, consonant or vowel in front. (This was an earlier rule: "Rob Hacked A Purse" for भक्तपुर failed it.)
2. **Write a real English sentence or scene,** with a little joke or picture in it. Real words, a grammatical shape, and ideally a person doing something odd: *Pie? Such Hyena!*, *Our car coach, or a hernia?*, *Call Lanky Johnny: 'Ow, Nose! Ow, Nose!'*, *Carlo, a shark, buys a barber.* The fun is that it sounds like normal English and then turns into Nepali.
3. **Do not just chop the spelling.** If you can rebuild the romanised answer by deleting the spaces and punctuation, the card is too thin. Each piece should be a word an English speaker would actually say.
4. **Do not leak the answer.** No piece of the Nepali word in plain sight, no other Nepali word (ama, didi, ghee, cup, chiya) and no translation of the answer.
5. **Match the sounds tightly.** Read it aloud fast in a Nepali accent and it must land on the answer, syllable for syllable. Weird breaks are good when consonants jump across the gaps (*Cut Ham And Ow!*), but a long card cannot drift.
6. **Prefer phrases to single words.** Things parents say, like questions, scolding, sayings and song lines, scored best. Use a one-word place or food only if the English picture is genuinely funny (*Moose Tongue*, *Lump Beanie*, *Barley Water*, *Bunny shower!*).
7. **Hardness comes from a picture that misleads, not from noise.** The best hard cards are long, clean, funny English that hides the Nepali well.

Some short cards were still loved: *Tea Edge*, *Ouch Are*, *Thus, Sign!*, *Thumb? Bah!*, *Deed? Oh!*. Short can work when the English words are real and make a strange little phrase. It just fails more often.
